# Is a 0.7384-Accuracy 3-Class Mammography Ensemble Near Its Ceiling? An Evidence Review

## Critical data-provenance flag

Every retrievable version of the Mammo-Bench paper — the medRxiv preprint ([medRxiv 2025.01.31.25321510](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.01.31.25321510v1)), its full-text EasyChair mirror ([EasyChair preprint 15792](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/DfFr/open)), and the Springer LNCS chapter version (DOI 10.1007/978-3-032-02489-3_11, [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/9ZPVWeb3/)) — describes Mammo-Bench as **74,436 images from 26,500 patients across 7 countries**, aggregating **seven** sources: DDSM, INbreast, KAU-BCMD, CMMD, CDD-CESM, DMID, and the RSNA Screening Dataset. No version describing "6 sources / 19,731 images / 6,500 patients / 6 countries" was found. The setup described in this analysis (five sources — DDSM, INbreast, KAU-BCMD, CMMD, DMID, with CDD-CESM filtered out) is a plausible subset of the published seven, and the RSNA Screening Dataset appears to have also been dropped versus what is described in the brief, but the exact provenance of the 19,731/6,500 figures could not be confirmed against any published version of the paper. This should be reconciled directly, since it affects how literally the Mammo-Bench numbers below can be treated as a same-population baseline rather than a closely-related but distinct one.

## Question 1 & 2: Is 0.7384 accuracy / 0.8928 macro-AUC consistent with the state of the art for patient-level, multi-source, 3-class classification?

### The core comparability problem in this literature

The published mammography classification literature splits into two populations that are not measuring the same difficulty. A large cluster of papers reports 90–99% accuracy, but essentially all of them share one or more of: image-level (not patient-level) splits, single small legacy datasets (MIAS, 322 original images; CBIS-DDSM alone), ROI-cropped lesion patches rather than full mammograms, or binary rather than 3-class framing. A much smaller cluster reports patient-level, multi-source, full-image 3-class results, and clusters in the 0.74–0.79 accuracy range. The gap between these two clusters is not primarily a modeling-quality gap — it is substantially an evaluation-protocol artifact.

Direct, quantified evidence for this comes from a 2026 study contrasting side-level and patient-level evaluation on the CMMD dataset across six backbones (ResNet-18/50, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet-121, ConvNeXt-Tiny, ViT-B/16) and three fusion strategies: side-level AUC exceeded patient-level AUC by **17.5 percentage points on average (range 12.7–22.4 pp)**, an inflation effect far larger than the under-3-point AUC spread between different architectures at fixed split protocol ([side-level vs. patient-level mammography evaluation, Taylor & Francis 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20565623.2026.2681575)). This is binary, single-source evidence, so it cannot be used for magnitude comparison to a 3-class task, but it is directly relevant methodological evidence that a large fraction of reported "very high accuracy" mammography results reflect leakage rather than genuine generalization.

### Triangulating a realistic accuracy ceiling

No single authoritative source states an explicit "realistic accuracy ceiling" for this exact task framing (patient-level, multi-source, full-image, 3-class). Triangulating across three independent proxies:

- **Mammo-Bench's own baseline** (0.778–0.788 accuracy) — the single most directly relevant anchor, though it is a non-ensembled baseline on what appears to be a somewhat larger/different source mix than the setup here ([Mammo-Bench](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/DfFr/open)).
- **Radiologist AUC** on curated reader-study data: 14 radiologists reading the NYU Reader Study Set (720 exams) achieved individual AUC ranging 0.705–0.860 (mean 0.778 ± 0.04), with mean AUC-PR of only 0.364 ± 0.05; several deep learning models (GMIC, DMV-CNN, GLAM) exceeded average radiologist AUC on this population ([NYU Reader Study analysis](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.04800)). Separately, a large real-world Australian BreastScreen cohort study reported AI AUC of 0.83 versus radiologist AUC of 0.93, with comparable sensitivity at matched thresholds (AI 0.67 vs. radiologist 0.68) ([Lancet eBioMedicine BreastScreen cohort study](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/ebiom/article/PIIS2352-3964(23)00063-4/fulltext)) — a detection task, not flat 3-class classification, but the 0.83–0.93 AUC band is broadly consistent with a macro ROC-AUC of 0.8928.
- **Inter-reader agreement as a ground-truth ceiling**: BI-RADS category agreement across readers is reported as only fair, kappa ≈ 0.37 in one study ([PubMed 22993385](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22993385/)), versus much higher agreement (kappa 0.86–0.94) for breast-density categories ([AJR study](https://ajronline.org/doi/10.2214/AJR.15.15049)). When ground-truth labels themselves carry only fair reader agreement, classifier accuracy is capped below what clean labels would allow — label noise becomes a co-dominant error source alongside model capacity.

Synthesizing these three lines of evidence points to a plausible realistic band of **high-0.70s to low-0.80s accuracy** for rigorously patient-level, multi-source, full-image 3-class mammography classification — this is a triangulated estimate, not a figure taken directly from one authoritative source, and should be treated with that caveat. **A result of 0.7384 accuracy sits at the lower-middle of that band, and a macro ROC-AUC of 0.8928 sits comfortably within (and toward the favorable end of) the radiologist-comparable AUC range.** This reading is consistent with — not evidence of underperforming — published state of the art for this exact task framing, while also indicating there is very likely a few points of accuracy still on the table before any genuine ceiling is approached, since the Mammo-Bench non-ensembled baseline (0.778–0.788) is itself higher than the ensemble result being evaluated here, even though the ensemble uses a more rigorous split protocol that would be expected to lower, not raise, the achievable number.

## Question 3: Is the cascade (hierarchical) classification finding replicated elsewhere?

### Mammo-Bench's own cascade result, verified against primary source

Mammo-Bench's two-stage cascade — a Normal-vs-Abnormal ResNet-101, followed by a second, separately trained Benign-vs-Malignant ResNet-101 applied only to predicted-Abnormal cases — reaches Stage 1 accuracy 0.891 (MCC 0.774) and Stage 2 accuracy 0.736 (MCC 0.479), with Stage 2 Benign F1 = 0.72 (precision 0.78, recall 0.67) against a flat 3-class Benign F1 of 0.450 with augmentation / 0.425 without — an absolute gain of **+0.27 F1 points** ([Mammo-Bench](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/DfFr/open)). Malignant F1 also improves modestly under the cascade (0.741 → 0.75).

### The broader literature shows a mixed, not universal, pattern

Two independent studies in adjacent breast/skin imaging domains found flat classification matching or beating a naive cascade on overall accuracy:

- A breast ultrasound (BUSI dataset) study comparing flat 3-class (Normal/Benign/Malignant) against a two-level cascade (Normal-vs-Tumor, then Benign-vs-Malignant) across multiple backbones found the best flat model (NASNet, 93.1% accuracy, F1 91.6%) marginally *outperformed* the best cascade combination (92.7% accuracy, F1 92.1%); the authors' own conclusion states the cascade did not significantly improve accuracy over the flat approach ([Kormpos et al., *Big Data and Cognitive Computing* 2025](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9c32/10f3ed35351cf0f20268a9568c7ea6d4d41d.pdf)).
- A dermoscopic skin-lesion study (4 classes: BCC, melanoma, nevus, pigmented benign keratosis) found flat SVM-RBF at 82.87% accuracy versus hierarchical classification at 81.80% — flat won, with routing errors at the first cascade stage compounding downstream ([Belal et al., *IJEAT* 2026](https://journals.blueeyesintelligence.org/index.php/ijeat/article/view/1006)).
- A chest X-ray COVID-19 study using a taxonomic hierarchy found the cascade improved the specific target class F1 (0.8889 vs. flat 0.8333, +0.056) but **hurt** macro-averaged F1 (0.5669 vs. flat 0.6491, −0.082) because minority leaf classes received F1 = 0 in the best hierarchical configuration ([Pereira et al., PMC7207172](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7207172/)) — an important caveat that cascades can trade aggregate performance for target-class gains, especially when a taxonomy fragments already-scarce classes further.

A separate mammography-specific study on VinDr-Mammo, CDD-CESM, and DMID did not run a flat-vs-cascade comparison, but showed that within a cascade, adding ROI/lesion-location stratification lifted the hardest (Benign-vs-Malignant) sub-task F1 from 0.4354/0.4330 (ResNet/EfficientNet, no ROI info) to 0.6023/0.6630/0.6667 (ResNet/EfficientNet/Swin, with ROI info) — confirming that the benign-vs-malignant distinction remains the hardest step even inside a cascade, and that auxiliary lesion-location information is a major lever independent of cascade structure itself ([Yoshitsugu, Kishimoto & Takemura, *Bioengineering* 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12938819/)).

### Synthesis

The reasonable read across this evidence is that **cascades help specifically when an early binary stage can reliably filter out easy majority cases**, leaving a cleaner, more separable residual problem for the harder distinction — as Mammo-Bench's 0.891-accuracy Normal-vs-Abnormal stage does — but cascades are not a universally superior architecture, and can underperform a well-tuned flat classifier when early-stage routing is unreliable or when the class taxonomy fragments scarce classes into even smaller leaf nodes. Typical reported gains, where cascades do win, cluster around **+0.05 to +0.27 on the specific target-class metric** (dermatology/COVID target-class F1: +0.056; mammography Benign F1: +0.27), with the size of the gain apparently scaling with how reliable the first-stage routing decision is.

## Question 4: Is the weak Benign class a documented, general property — and what fixes have evidence behind them?

### Yes — this is a well-documented, convergent finding, not an artifact of one pipeline

Three independent, mutually reinforcing mechanisms explain why Benign is consistently the hardest class:

**1. Genuine visual/radiological ambiguity.** Radiologists detect microcalcifications with over 95% sensitivity but their accuracy distinguishing *benign from malignant* microcalcifications is only around 70–75%, because both are frequently sub-millimeter, low-contrast, and morphologically similar, especially in dense tissue ([Nature Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27149-9)).

**2. Low inter-radiologist agreement precisely at benign-adjacent boundaries.** A study of 14 radiologists independently rating 40 breast-ultrasound lesions on the BI-RADS 0–5 scale found only fair agreement overall (kappa = 0.389); grouping into negative (BI-RADS 2–3) versus positive (BI-RADS 4–5) raised agreement to substantial (kappa = 0.612), but 24 of 40 cases showed total disagreement across all 14 readers, and one malignant case (medullary carcinoma) was rated benign by 8 of 14 readers — direct clinical evidence of benign/malignant visual overlap ([Calas et al., *Clinics* 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3275126/)). The same paper cites external comparison figures showing BI-RADS 4 (the most benign-adjacent "suspicious" category) kappa as low as **0.28** in one study, versus BI-RADS 3 kappa 0.72 and BI-RADS 5 kappa 0.60 — meaning ground truth itself is least reliable exactly where benign/malignant boundaries are drawn.

**3. Definitional heterogeneity of "benign" as a catch-all label.** Unlike Normal (relatively homogeneous) or Malignant (heterogeneous in subtype but sharing core malignant morphology), "Benign" spans pathologically distinct entities — fibroadenomas, cysts, fibrocystic change, papillomas — several of which are documented radiological mimickers of cancer ([Mimickers of breast malignancy, PMC8058137](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8058137/); [radiographic-pathologic discordance in benign masses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31561317/); [Understanding BI-RADS Category 3, RSNA](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/rg.240169); [B3 lesion diagnostic uncertainty](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/retrieve/095b3f54-80db-4d63-8e73-23db32fa3131/2025%20d_archi%20%20Navigating%20the%20Uncertainty%20of%20B3%20Breast%20Lesions%20Diagnostic%20Challenges%20and%20Evolving%20Management%20Strategies.pdf)). This heterogeneity compounds ordinary class-imbalance effects: Mammo-Bench's own single-source ablations show benign recall collapsing to 0.04–0.12 on several smaller constituent datasets ([Mammo-Bench](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/DfFr/open); general treatment in [PMC9818528](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9818528/)).

The strongest, most directly quantified levers for the benign class specifically are **cascade/hierarchical routing** (+0.27 F1 in the one mammography-specific study found), **auxiliary lesion-location/ROI information** (+0.17–0.23 F1 in a separate mammography study), and **margin-aware losses (LMF/LDAM family)** (up to +11.6 F1 points on the rarest class, though evidence here is from non-mammography medical imaging). Contrastive learning and Balanced-MixUp have plausible mechanisms but only qualitative support in the literature surveyed — treat those two as promising but unproven for the benign class specifically. No mammography-specific quantitative results for the original LDAM or Class-Balanced Loss formulations were located; that is a genuine gap in the literature, not evidence they don't work.

## Question 5: Multi-view (CC+MLO) fusion — documented gains

Across architectures reporting a clean single-view-vs-fused-view ablation on the matched backbone and dataset, CC+MLO fusion produces a **consistently positive but architecture- and saturation-dependent gain**, ranging from near-zero to roughly 5–12 percentage points depending on metric and how close the single-view baseline already is to ceiling.

| Architecture | Dataset | Split / classes | Single-view baseline | Fused result | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [MV-Swin-T, Sarker et al., ISBI 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450559/) | CBIS-DDSM Mass | Binary; split-level not explicit | Acc 59.64% / AUC 60.26% | Acc 68.63% / AUC 71.37% | **+8.99 pp acc, +11.11 pp AUC** |
| MV-Swin-T | CBIS-DDSM Mass&Calc | Binary | Acc 60.46% / AUC 61.28% | Acc 65.37% / AUC 66.43% | **+4.91 pp acc, +5.15 pp AUC** |
| MV-Swin-T | VinDr-Mammo | Binary (BI-RADS 1–3 vs 4–6) | Acc 95.50% / AUC 95.88% | Acc 95.50% / AUC 96.08% | ~0 pp (near-saturated dataset) |
| [MamT⁴, Ibragimov et al., arXiv:2411.01669](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01669v1) | VinDr-Mammo | Exam-level 4000/1000; binary | AUC 79.6±2.0, F1 44.7±0.4 | AUC 84.0±1.7, F1 56.0±1.3 | **+4.4 pp AUC, +11.3 pp F1** |
| [MVPT-NET, Chen & Martel, arXiv:2504.19900](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19900v1) | OPTIMAM (4 UK NHS sites, multi-institution) | Subject-level 80/20, 5-fold CV; **3-class** (benign/DCIS/invasive) | Acc 0.668±0.010 / AUROC 0.831±0.006 / F1 0.664±0.011 | Acc 0.688±0.007 / AUROC 0.852±0.004 / F1 0.683±0.009 | **+2.0 pp acc, +2.1 pp AUROC, +1.9 pp F1** |
| [MammoFusion-Net, Chakravarthy et al., MethodsX 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937147/) | VinDr-Mammo | Case-level, stratified 70:30 + 5-fold CV; binary | ResNet18 single-view Acc 83.68% / AUC 78.34% | Naive mixed-view Acc 88.38% / AUC 85.38%; full proposed model Acc 92.12% / AUC 89.79% | **+4.70 pp acc (naive concat); +17.8 pp acc total with full attention-based fusion** |

The most externally valid single estimate for a task resembling this setup is the **MVPT-NET 3-class result on the multi-institution OPTIMAM cohort with a proper subject-level split: roughly +2.0 pp accuracy and +2.1 pp AUROC** from adding view fusion within the same framework — a large, multi-site, subject-level evaluation is a stronger generalization signal than single-site academic benchmarks like CBIS-DDSM, where dataset-specific artifacts can inflate apparent gains. MammoFusion-Net's staged ablation is the strongest evidence that *architecture* matters as much as the presence of a second view: naive concatenation alone captures roughly a third of the total available gain, and attention-based gating captures the rest.

Named architectures with only thin or non-quantitative evidence: **MammoInsight** is explicitly self-described by its own authors as a preliminary, development-stage framework with no split protocol, class count, or single-vs-multi-view numbers reported anywhere ([JAIIO/CAIS](https://revistas.unlp.edu.ar/JAIIO/article/download/18555/18284/83103)) — it should not be treated as evidence either way. **Cross-view attention approaches** (the originating "Cross-View Attention Network," [Zhao, Yu & Wang, ICASSP 2020](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Cross-View-Attention-Network-for-Breast-Cancer-from-Zhao-Yu/f2ff54eaa0a3debb1436c369312f689d562852a1)) have only a qualitative claim of improvement over non-attention and single-view models, with no exact numbers recoverable. A multi-source (six-dataset) generalization study confirms both simple-concatenation and dual-branch fusion beat single-view baselines but does not report the baseline numbers themselves, so the magnitude cannot be quantified from that source, though it does show that dual-branch, late-fusion designs generalize more robustly across unseen datasets/scanners than early-fusion, spatially concatenated designs ([Abdikenov et al., *J Imaging* 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12387149/)).

A practical implementation risk worth flagging: multi-view networks can develop a learning imbalance where one view (commonly MLO, which typically shows more tissue) dominates gradient updates, requiring shared weights between view branches or explicit view/modality dropout as mitigation.

## Question 6: Effect of input resolution, and is 512px a bottleneck?

Two well-controlled mammography-specific resolution ablations both point the same direction — higher resolution reliably improves AUC, with an effect size large enough to matter:

- Varying effective pixel spacing while holding patch size fixed at 512px, moving from 200 to 100 µm/pixel raised AUC by **+0.028 on CBIS-DDSM and +0.105 on an internal dataset (GEHC)**; combining multiple resolutions in an ensemble beat every single resolution tested (CBIS-DDSM AUC 0.789 vs. best single-resolution 0.764) ([Quintana et al., *Bioengineering* 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10215225/)). Critically, this paper's lesion-size-stratified analysis found that downsampling **disproportionately hurts small lesions** — the size class microcalcifications fall into.
- Moving from quarter-resolution to full-resolution (2200×1600) synthetic mammograms raised macro AUC by **+0.113**; the authors explicitly state they avoided more aggressive downsampling specifically to preserve "finer features that may represent early cancer or ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)" ([SCREENet, Seyyedi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.08563v2.pdf)) — a direct, first-party statement connecting resolution to microcalcification/DCIS-scale detail.

However, the relationship is **not simply monotonic**. A CBIS-DDSM ablation spanning 224/512/1024/2048px on whole mammograms found 512px beat 224px by +2.03 to +4.52 percentage points accuracy depending on architecture, but going from 512px to 1024px *without* added preprocessing or architecture changes **hurt** accuracy by −1.76 points due to overfitting; only 2048px combined with filtering, histogram equalization, and cropping recovered gains (+3.70 points over baseline) ([Jaamour et al., *PLOS ONE* 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0280841)). The authors state plainly that "larger image sizes do not yield increased accuracies without image preprocessing" — naively upscaling resolution on an existing pipeline risks *hurting* performance unless paired with expanded network capacity and preprocessing changes.

On whether 512px is a documented "bottleneck": a 2025 master's thesis states directly that state-of-the-art models "reduce training images to 512×512 pixels or less because of technical and computational-memory limitations," causing "loss of large amounts of structural detail" and models "missing small tumors that could be visible in a high-resolution version of the same image" ([Kujala, Oulu 2025](https://oulurepo.oulu.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/59195/nbnfioulu-202511146721.pdf?sequence=1)) — this is authoritative in direction but **not independently quantified** with a controlled resolution-accuracy table in that thesis. No paper reviewed provides a controlled, quantified accuracy/AUC ablation isolating microcalcification-only cases across multiple resolutions in mammography specifically — the microcalcification-resolution link is well-supported qualitatively and semi-quantitatively (lesion-size-stratified harm from downsampling, explicit DCIS-preservation design rationale) but the specific magnitude of a microcalcification-detection gain from higher resolution is not established numerically anywhere found.

The field's most common response to this tradeoff is not uniform upscaling but a **global-low-resolution + local-high-resolution-patch** design: GMIC processes the whole image at reduced resolution for global context while separately extracting full-resolution local patches (256×256px) around salient regions identified by a saliency map, evaluated on the very large NYU Breast Cancer Screening Dataset (229,426 exams, patient-level 80/10/10 split) plus CBIS-DDSM ([Shen, Wu et al., *Medical Image Analysis* 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7828643/)). Its 3D extension reports that this design uses **77.98–90.05% less GPU memory and 91.23–96.02% less computation** than naively running an off-the-shelf high-resolution CNN on the whole volume, while retaining fine local detail ([3D-GMIC, Park et al., arXiv:2210.08645](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.08645)) — this is likely the most computationally practical route to capturing microcalcification-relevant detail without the overfitting risk documented in the PLOS ONE ablation.

The ranking weighs both magnitude and the strength/directness of supporting evidence: cascade classification is ranked first because its Benign-F1 gain is verified on essentially the same dataset family as this setup, even though its effect on *overall* accuracy is less certain given mixed replication elsewhere. Multi-view fusion is ranked second on the strength of a conservative, multi-institution, 3-class estimate. Loss-function changes are ranked relatively high on effort-adjusted basis because they are the cheapest intervention with quantified (if not mammography-specific) minority-class gains. Naive resolution scaling is ranked last among "try next" items because the one directly comparable ablation shows it can actively hurt accuracy without accompanying architectural changes.

## Question 8: Do CNN ensembles saturate on medical imaging tasks — is a +0.67pp gain low?

A NeurIPS 2023 theoretical framework formalizes when ensembling helps: it defines the Ensemble Improvement Rate (relative error-rate reduction) and shows it is tightly coupled to the Disagreement-Error Ratio (ratio of pairwise prediction disagreement to average member error) — "one classifier is often enough whenever the disagreement rate is low relative to the average error rate." The paper further shows that modern, high-capacity "interpolating" deep networks trained to near-zero training error (the regime ResNet-101 and EfficientNet-B5 typically operate in) consistently exhibit **lower disagreement and correspondingly lower ensemble improvement** than non-interpolating or lower-capacity models — deep-ensemble experiments show gains becoming "much less pronounced" once models pass the interpolation threshold, and fine-tuned BERT ensembles on GLUE tasks (also a high-capacity, near-interpolating regime) showed uniformly low improvement across all seven tasks tested ([When are ensembles really effective?, NeurIPS 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/30b6fa308e62ed52180c31ae3ba6bb0a-Paper-Conference.pdf)). This directly predicts that an ensemble of three high-capacity, single-team-trained CNN/self-supervised-CNN backbones on a shared dataset — with likely correlated failure modes from shared class-imbalance and domain-shift challenges — should sit toward the low end of possible ensemble gains.

Documented empirical gains, for calibration:

| Study | Domain | Gain from ensembling | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [CheXzero, PMC9792370](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9792370/) | Chest X-ray, zero-shot | **+1.1 pp AUC** (checkpoint-averaging, same architecture) | Low-diversity ensembling strategy |
| [DREAM Mammography Challenge](https://web.stanford.edu/group/rubinlab/pubs/Schaffter-2020-EvaluationCombinedArtifici.pdf), primary test set | Binary mammography cancer detection | **+3.7 pp AUC** (best team 0.858 → stacked ensemble of 8 independently-developed teams 0.895) | High-diversity: independently developed models from different teams |
| Same challenge, Karolinska subset | Same | **+2.0 pp AUC** (0.903 → 0.923) | Gain magnitude is population-dependent even within the same challenge |
| [Stacking in medical imaging, arXiv 2201.11440](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11440) | General medical imaging, multiple tasks | Up to **+13 pp F1** via stacking (upper-bound/best-case range) | Not a single homogeneous result |
| [Radiologist ensembling, RSNA AI](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/ryai.2019190187) | Human-reader ensembling (not model) | **+10 pp (2 readers), +18 pp (4 readers)** sensitivity | Genuinely independent human readers — a high-diversity analogue, structurally different from correlated CNN checkpoints |

A gain of roughly +0.67 pp accuracy (best single model 0.7317 → ensemble 0.7384) is **below** even the CheXzero same-architecture-checkpoint case (+1.1 pp AUC) and well below the DREAM Challenge's cross-team gains (+2.0 to +3.7 pp AUC), despite the ensemble here having architecturally distinct members. Given the NeurIPS 2023 theory's prediction that high-capacity, near-interpolating, single-team-trained ensembles fall into a low-disagreement, low-improvement regime, **a sub-1pp gain is consistent with, not anomalous relative to, both the theoretical framework and the empirical pattern for this class of ensemble** — it is broadly comparable in order of magnitude to the CheXzero checkpoint-averaging case, and the larger gains documented in the literature (2.0–18 pp) all come from genuinely higher-diversity scenarios: independently developed multi-team models or independent human readers, not architecture variants trained by one team on one dataset. No mammography-specific 3-class CNN-ensemble-gain figure was found in the literature searched, so this assessment is triangulated from adjacent-domain evidence and general ensemble theory rather than a direct mammography precedent — that absence of a direct precedent should be treated as a genuine evidence gap, not resolved by the adjacent-domain analogy alone.

## Summary of evidence gaps and limitations (stated explicitly per requirement)

- The exact dataset-composition discrepancy (7-source/74,436-image/26,500-patient Mammo-Bench as published, versus the 6-source/19,731-image/6,500-patient description in this setup) could not be resolved from any retrievable version of the paper and should be reconciled directly.
- No mammography-specific 3-class ensemble-gain figure, and no mammography-specific LDAM or Class-Balanced Loss quantitative result, were located in the literature searched — both are genuine gaps, not negative findings.
- No paper provides a controlled, quantified accuracy/AUC ablation isolating microcalcification-only cases across multiple input resolutions in mammography; the resolution-microcalcification link is well-supported qualitatively and semi-quantitatively (lesion-size-stratified harm, explicit DCIS-preservation design rationale) but not numerically established.
- No single authoritative source states an explicit "realistic accuracy ceiling" for patient-level, multi-source, full-image, 3-class mammography classification; the high-0.70s-to-low-0.80s band presented here is triangulated from Mammo-Bench's baseline, radiologist AUC studies, and inter-reader kappa studies, not quoted directly from one source.
- Evidence for contrastive learning and Balanced-MixUp on the benign/minority class specifically is qualitative only in the sources located, and should not be treated as equivalent in strength to the quantified LMF/LDAM or ROI-supervision findings.
- The 2026 dates on several cited sources (CMMD side-vs-patient-level study, Swin-DAMFN, Belal et al., Yoshitsugu et al.) reflect genuinely recent publications found during this research; their findings have not yet had time to accumulate independent replication in the literature.
